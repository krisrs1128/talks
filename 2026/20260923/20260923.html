Motivation

  1. Comparison is fundamental to biological discovery. In the sequencing era, our comparisons are often based on large-scale molecular readouts.

    • In functional genomics, we compare molecular profiles before and after knocking out genes.
    • In cancer biology, we compare molecular profiles in cancer versus healthy tissue.
    • In microbiome studies, we compare microbial species communities before and after treatments.
    • In single cell eQTL studies, we look for cell-type-specific expression profiles that differs across genotypes.

    Viewed more abstractly, this is a problem of comparing the frequency of genomic features across conditions, potentially accounting for secondary factors, like timepoints or patient characteristics, that can also influence the observed frequencies.

  2. The overarching goal of differential expression analysis is to identify genomic features that vary systematically across conditions of interest. From an analysis perspective, there are two fundamental challenges:

    • The “usual” statistical assumptions do not immediately apply.
    • There may be thousands of features of potential interest. The more we analyze, the greater the risk that irrelevant features appear associated just by chance.

    The first problem motivates normalization methods, and the second motivates multiple hypothesis testing methods. There have been many proposals for both normalization and multiple hypothesis testing. We will focus on the specific procedure adopted by limma, though many of the ideas of this approach appear throughout the literature.

Linear Models Background

  1. The machinery that underlies both normalization and multiple hypothesis testing is the linear model. We briefly detour to review the mechanics of hypothesis testing through linear models. There will be math, but the math is necessary to make our understanding precise.

  2. Let \(y_n\) represent gene expression levels across samples \(n\). Imagine that data come from two groups (e.g., healthy and disease, or knockout and control) and that are both Gaussian distributed. The groups may have different means \(\mu\) but are required to have the same variances \(\sigma^2\).

    \[ \begin{align} y_{n} \sim \begin{cases} \mathcal{N}\left(\mu_{A}, \sigma^2\right) & \text{for } n \in \text{Group A} \\ \mathcal{N}\left(\mu_{B}, \sigma^2\right) & \text{for } n \in \text{Group B} \end{cases} \end{align} \]

    If we want to see whether the true (unobserved) group means are the same, we can compute,

    \[ t = \frac{\bar{y}_A - \bar{y}_B}{s\sqrt{1/N_A + 1/N_B}}. \]

    This is the differences in observed means of groups A and B, adjusted according to an empirical estimate \(s^2\) of the within-group variance1. This adjustment accounts for the spread within each group when interpreting the difference in means.

  3. We will say that when the means of the two groups are equal, the null hypothesis holds. The reason for the naming is that when the two groups have the same mean, there is no need to further examine subgroup differences (null \(\iff\) nothing more to look at). Formally, this is denoted,

    \[H_{0}: \mu_{A} = \mu_{B}\]

    Even when \(H_{0}\) holds, the observed difference in sample means will be nonzero on any actual dataset, and therefore \(t \neq 0\). In the plot below, the normal densities denote the true data distributions in each group, the points represent the observed data, and the horizontal lines reflect the observed sample means.

    However, the further the statistic \(t\) is from zero (either negative or positive), the less plausible the null hypothesis \(H_0\) becomes. The example below is a case where \(H_{0}\) does not hold, and this is reflected in the relatively larger \(t\)-statistic.

  4. Under the null hypothesis \(H_{0}\), what would be typical values of \(t\)? We can answer this with a simulation. I’ve simulated 5K null datasets, computed the associated \(t\)-statistics, and put those statistics into a histogram. These turn out to follow a clean distribution2, and it’s in fact possible to derive a theoretical formula for it, which is overlaid.

  5. This is useful because it tells us that anything that doesn’t fall into this distribution is evidence against the null hypothesis. I generated one sample where the true group difference is 2 and I’ve overlaid the \(t\)-statistic from that dataset onto the null from before.

    This is the essential logic of hypothesis testing: if we see a statistic that doesn’t fall into the bulk of the null distribution, then we have evidence that the null hypothesis is false. A \(p\)-value is defined as the area of the distribution with larger magnitude than the test statistic, and it is a measure of the strength of this evidence (smaller area \(\to\) less likely to come from the null).

  6. So if we’re willing to believe the Gaussian assumption, we have a procedure for comparing means across two groups. But in genomics, we often want to analyze several factors at once, either because we have several treatments to give simultaneously or because we need to control for secondary factors. For many centuries, the guiding principle was to analyze one factor at a time, varying one but fixing all the others. This would mean working with separate subsets of data.

  7. One of the major insights of statistics is that it can be beneficial to analyze multiple factors at once, because it allows you to share evidence across all subsets3. The idea is to proceed like in Figure 4, where we care about the difference between A and B, but the response is influenced by a secondary factor \(x_n\). If we subsampled into data within a narrow range of \(x_n\), then we would be discarding most of the information. Conversely, if we ignored this factor and only compared the \(y_n\) values, then the groups would become more blended and we would struggle to detect a difference.

  8. In this case we might want to test the null hypothesis \(H_{0}\) that the lines for the two groups are exactly overlapping. The main trick is to subtract out the effects from the \(x_n\) and see whether there still remains a difference between the two groups. That is, after subtracing the \(x_n\) effects, we return to the setting above.

    This is exactly what a linear model does. The formula becomes more complicated, but we can again derive a \(t\)-statistic that has a closed form null distribution under \(H_{0}\).

  9. The fact that we can make comparisons between groups while accounting for the influence of many factors is what makes linear models such a versatile statistical method. The example here uses a straight line for \(x_n\) but the same machinery works generally, including when the effects are nonlinear, when the slopes of the lines are not parallel, and when we have a treatment with more than two levels.

Normalization

  1. Unfortunately the Gaussian assumption doesn’t make sense for genomics data. But the literature has found ways to salvage their applicability. This is done is through a process called normalization, and it lies at the heart of limma.

  2. There are two issues that stand in the way of making the Gaussian assumption. The first is that sequencing assays return counts of different genomic features, and these are not continuous measurements like the Gaussian. The second is that each sample’s mean can depend on its sequencing depth. Sequencing depth is a technical factor that can obscure the actual biological differences between conditions.

  3. An idea that addresses both problems is the log counts-per-million (CPM) transformation. Suppose that the expression for gene \(g\) in sample \(n\) is \(y_{ng}\). Arrange all these data into a samples-by-genes matrix4 \(Y \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times G}\). The logCPM transformation is,

    \[ y_{ng} \rightsquigarrow \log\left(\frac{y_{ng} + \frac{1}{2}}{\sum_{k}y_{nk} + 1} \times 10^{6}\right) \]

    The \(\frac{1}{2}\) and \(1\) offfsets help us avoid \(\log\left(0\right)\), which is undefined. Besides this, the fraction inside of the log converts each sample into something like a relative frequency of gene \(g\) in sample \(n\) (it would be exactly this without the offsets). This removes differences in sequencing depths. The \(10^6\) is where the “per-million” name comes from. Finally, by taking the log transformation, we convert discrete counts into a continuous scale where the normal distribution assumption is more believable.

  4. The three genes in Figure 6 are shown before and after applying the CPM transformation. The data are described in more detail in the code example below. For XIST, the Gaussian approximation makes much more sense after the transformation.

    At this point, we can, in principle, fit a linear model where the experimental factors are features \(x_{n}\) and the transformed gene expression is the response \(y_n\).

  5. But there are still issues. When we convert to frequencies, we’re losing a meaningful information about the precision of our measurements. Two samples can have exactly the same logCPM value for a gene but different total counts. The total count information is important because a sample with deep sequencing is measured more accurately than one with shallow sequencing.

  6. This shows up in the spread of the groups after the logCPM transformation has been applied. For example, in Figure 75, the logCPM transformation has put all the genes on roughly the same scale. Instead of ranging from 0 to 40,000, they’re in roughly the range from -5 to 10.

    But notice that the ZBTB16 gene has more variation within each group compared to the other two genes. In statistical language, this is called the residual variation (after residualizing out the group effect), and it happens to be a general phenomenon that genes with smaller sequencing depth also have larger residual variation after the logCPM transformation.

  7. Limma uses this trend to reweight the observations. The curve above was learned using gene-level quantities. But we can compute the quantity on the x-axis for any count \(y_{ng}\), and this suggests an associated weight, called the precision weight. Intuitively, if an observation comes from a sample with deep sequencing, it will have higher precision.

    These weights can then be incorporated into linear models through weighted linear regression. For example in the simple case where we’re simply looking at the difference between two groups, we would compute a weighted average instead of a plain average.

Multiple Hypothesis Testing

  1. At this point, we could imagine applying a linear model to every single genomic feature. We would have a separate null hypothesis associated with each gene. We would reject those where the groups means seem different (controlling for other factors). The resulting list of significant genes would be the ones that we prioritize for follow-up analysis.

  2. This doesn’t quite work, though. It’s easiest to understand through a small example. I’ve simulated a thousand hypothetical genes from two groups. For every gene, I simulated variances \(\sigma_{g}^{2}\) from a uniform distribution between 0.5 and 2, so some have expression that stay closer to their mean than others. For 50 of these genes, I introduce a true difference of 2.5 between the means6. In the language of the two-groups model above, \(\mu_{A} = 0, \mu_{B} = 2.5\). I sample only two replicates per group (four samples total).

  3. Figure 9a shows the 50 genes with the most significant \(t\)-statistics. Only 11 are actually related to the treatment! Of the top 10 genes, only two are actually relevant.

    Figure 9b is the critical clue. The genes that appear most significant actually just have estimated variances \(s_{g}^2\) that are smaller than their true variances \(\sigma_{g}^2\), which we know in the simulation, but which we wouldn’t know in reality. The problem is that with small sample sizes, we might have a small empirical variance just by chance, and since the \(t\)-statistic divides by \(s_g\), we end up overestimating the associated genes’ significance.

    \[ t_{g} = \frac{\bar{y}_{A,g} - \bar{y}_{B,g}}{s_{g}\sqrt{1/N_A + 1/N_B}}. \]

  4. When we observe a small \(s_{g}^2\), it could either be because the gene truly has small variation around its mean, or we just got replicates that are unusually close to each other. The major insight of limma is that the other genes can help us tell which of these two situations we are in.

    To this end, suppose we had a prior \(H\) for the genewise variances, \[ \sigma_{g}^{2} \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} H \] This captures our prior knowledge about how much variation is expected in each group. Since the distribution of \(s_{g}^2\) given \(\sigma_{g}^{2}\) has a known form, it is possible to compute a posterior for each gene \(g\), \[ p\left(\sigma_{g}^2 \vert s_{g}^2\right). \] This posterior gives a better estimate of \(\sigma_{g}^2\) which we can use in place of \(s_{g}^2\). Figure 10 shows the prior-to-posterior update for gene YWHAB, which had the smallest estimated \(s_{g}^2\).

    How did limma choose the prior \(H\)? It turns out that it is possible to estimate \(H\) by looking at the \(s_{g}^2\) across all the genes. This is what is meant by limma performing empirical Bayes. Compared to standard Bayesian inference, where the prior must simply be assumed, we can use the many parallel genes to form an “empirical” prior \(\hat{H}\), which can then flow into the posterior calculations.

  5. A consequence of these updated \(\sigma_{g}^2\) estimates is that the new, “moderated” \(t\)-statistics are less affected by small values of \(s_{g}^2\). Looking across all genes, Figure 11 shows that the \(t\)-statistics are shrunken towards zero. The genes that are shrunken the most are those whose \(s_{g}^2\) were much lower than expected after estimating the prior variance \(H\).

    When using these \(t\)-statistics instead of the original ones, we have fewer false positives among our top-ranked genes. For example, returning to our earlier simulation, we can see that ranking using the moderated \(t\)-statistics leads to many fewer false positives and is nearly as good as using the ground truth standard deviation.

  6. There’s one last statistical consideration. If we use the usual hypothesis testing cutoffs then we’re liable to return many false positives…

    The problem is that the hypothesis testing cutoffs were originally designed so that, under the null hypothesis, the probability of a false positive is \(\alpha\) (usually 0.05). But in modern biology we test many hypothesis, and the probability of encountering at least one false positive grows with the number of tests.

  7. To deal with this phenomenon (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995) had the idea that instead of controlling the probability of a single false positive, we should control the proportion of false positives among all rejections. Formally, let \(H_{1}, \dots, H_{G}\) represent the null hypothesis for the \(G\) genes. Suppose we obtain p-values \(p_{1}, \dots, p_{G}\) and that \(R\) of these \(p\)-values are smaller than \(\alpha\). The \(R\) rejections are the discoveries we would comment on in our paper. Among them, suppose that \(V\) are false discoveries (in practice, we would never know this). The quantity

    \[ \widehat{\mathrm{FDP}} = \frac{V}{R\vee 1} \] is the empirical false discovery proportion in this one experiment and its expected value across many experiments with the same underlying distribution is called the false discovery rate, \[ \mathrm{FDR} = \mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\mathrm{FDP}}\right] \]

  8. In addition to introducing this quantity, Benjamini and Hochberg proposed a way of adjusting the \(p\)-value cutoffs \(\alpha_{k}\) so that if we reject hypotheses using these cutoffs instead of \(\alpha\), we would provably control the false discovery rate. Their procedure is now called the Benjamini-Hochberg (or BH) procedure, and the thresholds \(\alpha_{k}\) are plotted as the magenta line in panel (B) below.

    • Sort the \(G\) p-values in increasing order, \[ p_{(1)} \le p_{(2)} \le \cdots \le p_{(G)} \] and denote the associated nulls by \(H_{(1)}, \dots, H_{(G)}\).
    • Fix an FDR target \(q\)7 and define new thresholds \(\alpha_{k} = \frac{kq}{G}\). This means that the smallest p-value is compared to the strictest threshold, the next smallest is compared to a slightly more lenient one, etc.
    • Find the largest \(k\) where \(p_{(k)} \le \alpha_{k}\), and reject \(H_{(1)}, \dots, H_{(k)}\). If there is no such \(k\), don’t reject anything.

  9. This procedure can be justified through the histogram of \(p\)-values, like those in panel (A) above. These \(p\)-values can be intuitively understood as a mixture of those from significant genes (large peak with small \(p\)-values) and those from nulls (everything else). Let \(\pi_{0}\) denote the histogram density after the peak levels off. When many of the hypotheses are null, then the peak would be smaller and \(\pi_{0}\) would be larger8.

    Normalize the histogram to have the total area of 1, so each gene has weight \(\frac{1}{G}\). If we let \(R\left(t\right)\) give the total number of rejections below a threshold \(t\), the corresponding area to the left of this histogram is \(\frac{R\left(t\right)}{G}\).

    How much of this area do we think comes from the null hypotheses? A reasonable estimate is \(\pi_{0}t\).

    So, a good estimate of the false discovery rate is the second area divided by the first,

    \[ \mathrm{FDR}\left(t\right) \approx \frac{G\pi_{0}t}{R\left(t\right)}. \]

  10. How might we choose \(t\) so that the FDR is controlled below a target level \(q\)? Suppose we set \(t\) to the \(k^{th}\) largest \(p\)-value, \(p_{\left(k\right)}\). Then we would have \(k\) rejections, i.e. \(R\left(t\right) = k\). Substituting this into \(t\) and \(R\left(t\right)\), we find,

    \[ \begin{align} \mathrm{FDR}\left(t\right) \leq q \iff \frac{G \pi_{0} p_{\left(k\right)}}{k} \leq q \iff p_{\left(k\right)} \leq \frac{kq}{G \pi_{0}}. \end{align} \]

    Approximating9 \(\pi_{0} \approx 1\) (i.e., most genes are null), the right hand side is \(\alpha_{k}\). So, if we want to reject as many hypothesis as possible while controlling the FDR below \(q\), then we should choose the largest \(p_{\left(k\right)} \leq \alpha_{k}\). This is exactly the BH procedure.

  11. This isn’t exactly a proof, but hopefully it gives some intuition about where the BH procedure came from. Finally, while it’s nice to know where the multiple testing correction comes from, we never have to compute the Benjamini-Hochberg thresholds ourselves. When we run topTable, limma automatically performs this multiple testing correction.

  2. As before, the first step is to filter genes that have low expression. This reduces the data from 38410 to 18189 rows (we have so many genes because RNA-seq picks up on the noncoding RNAs).

  3. Next we apply the logCPM normalization and recover expression weights. This depends on the design because the residual variances are computed after the initial regression onto sex.

    design <- model.matrix(~sex, data = dge$samples)
v <- voom(dge, design)

  4. The weights and original design are passed to the linear model, and the variances are shrunk towards the prior mean in the empirical Bayes step.

    fit_raw <- lmFit(v, design)
fit <- eBayes(fit_raw)

    Plotting the original vs. moderated \(t\)-statistics shows that, with more samples, the empirical bayes shrinkage step has less effect than it did in the airway dataset.

Learning More

  1. All the figures in this notebook are created using code that’s directly executed inline. All the statistical quantities, like the moderated t-statistics and the prior and posterior variances, were extracted from limma. You can rerun our source code to get hands-on experience, and I encourage you to try making these kinds of plots on your own data.

  2. You can find many more code examples in the Limma online manual.

  3. Limma is just one strategy for multiple testing with RNA-seq data. Another Bioconductor-based approach is DESeq2 (Love et al. 2014). It applies generalized linear models rather than linear models, which is a bit more conceptually advanced. But the software is also well developed and the key concepts like normalization, empirical Bayesian shrinkage, and multiple testing all carry over.

  4. The simulation in the multiple testing section was inspired by a similar one in (Ji and Liu 2010). It also motivates a Bayesian approach to sharing information across genes. A deep Empirical Bayesian analysis of the limma is given by (Ignatiadis and Sen 2025). What the original limma paper presents as a somewhat ad hoc recipe this paper shows is an immediate consequence of some basic (and reasonable) statistical assumptions.

  5. Chapter 6 of (Holmes and Huber 2019) gives a great introduction to multiple testing in high-throughput omics, and it’s available for free online.
